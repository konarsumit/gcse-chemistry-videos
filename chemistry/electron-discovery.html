What happened in 1897

Thomson took a glass tube, pumped nearly all the gas out of it, and passed a very high voltage (around 15,000 volts) between two electrodes at either end. A stream of glowing rays shot out from the negative electrode, the cathode, and struck the glass at the far end, making it glow.

At the time nobody knew what these "cathode rays" actually were. Thomson tested them with electric and magnetic fields and found something surprising:

  • The rays always bent toward the positively charged plate, meaning they carried a negative charge.
  • The amount they bent was the same no matter what gas was in the tube or what metal the electrodes were made of.
  • By measuring the deflection, Thomson calculated the charge-to-mass ratio of the particles and found they were roughly 1,800 times lighter than a hydrogen atom.

This told him the rays were not light, and not whole atoms, but a stream of tiny negatively charged particles that exist inside every atom. He called them "corpuscles" — today we call them electrons. It was the first evidence that the atom was not the smallest, indivisible particle of matter, as had been assumed for centuries.
